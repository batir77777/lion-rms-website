Fifteen things to look at on a fire door, a place to record what you found and
what you did about it, and a defects and actions log. Each door gets its own
sheet, so a completed record can be filed, reprinted or handed on by itself.

## What this checklist is for

To give someone doing a routine walk-round a consistent set of things to look at
on a <GlossaryLink slug="fire-door">fire door</GlossaryLink>, and somewhere to
write down what they saw — between the formal inspections that a competent
person carries out.

It is built around the person actually doing it: someone walking a corridor with
a clipboard, looking at doors one at a time. Every line is written so that **Y is
the satisfactory answer**, and each line is answered in one of three separate
boxes — **Y**, **N** or **N/A** — with one mark per line. Three boxes rather than
one means an unanswered line is visibly incomplete rather than quietly reading as
"no".

Below the fifteen lines, each door has a short conclusion block: whether a defect
was found, whether the defect is urgent, what was done about it and who it was
reported to, and — the line that matters most — **whether anything about this
door requires competent advice**. That question is there for the case where all
fifteen lines are satisfactory and the door still does not look right to the
person standing in front of it. A checklist with nowhere to put that judgement
loses it.

## Who it is for

<GlossaryLink slug="responsible-person">Responsible persons</GlossaryLink> under
the
<LegislationLink slug="regulatory-reform-fire-safety-order-2005">Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005</LegislationLink>,
housing providers, managing agents, facilities managers and caretakers carrying
out routine visual condition checks of fire doors.

It is written to be usable by a competent member of staff on a walk-round. It is
explicitly **not** written for, and is not adequate for, a competent-person fire
door inspection or a fire door survey.

## What it does not do

This is a **general template**, and the limitations are the load-bearing part of
it.

This is a **routine visual check**. It is not a fire door inspection by a
competent person, and it is emphatically **not a fire door survey**. A visual
check of a door in place cannot tell you about the door's construction, its core,
its certification, the integrity of its frame fixings, the condition of concealed
intumescent seals, whether the right ironmongery was used, or whether the
installation was ever correct in the first place. Those questions need someone
competent, with the right access, and often invasive inspection.

It does not replace, and must not be presented as:

- a competent fire risk assessment;
- professional fire-engineering advice;
- **a fire door survey or a competent-person fire door inspection**;
- statutory inspection, testing or maintenance by a
  <GlossaryLink slug="competent-person">competent person</GlossaryLink>;
- premises-specific legal advice.

**A completed sheet with every box ticked is not evidence that the doors are
compliant.** It is evidence that somebody looked at them and saw nothing
obviously wrong. Those are very different claims, which is why the caution is
printed at the top of page one and in the footer of every page rather than in
small print at the end. A door can satisfy every line on this sheet and still not
perform in a fire.

Whether your checking arrangements are the right ones for your building is a
question for your
<GlossaryLink slug="fire-risk-assessment">fire risk assessment</GlossaryLink>, not
for this template. No inspection or checking interval is stated anywhere in this
resource.

## A note on gaps

The checklist asks whether the gaps around the leaf and at the threshold are free
from anything excessive, uneven or visibly abnormal, and whether daylight shows
around or beneath a closed door where it should not. It does not give you a
figure to measure against, and **no line asks for a measurement**. That is a
deliberate decision, not an omission.

A number on a general template invites a compliance judgement that a visual check
cannot support: a user with a gauge and a published figure would conclude they
had measured compliance, and a user without one would guess. Either outcome turns
a walk-round into an apparent certification of the door set, its installation and
its fire resistance — the exact misreading this resource exists to prevent. If a
door needs measuring, that is a job for a competent person.

## What the fifteen lines cover

In outline: whether the door is being held open by anything; whether it closes
fully onto the frame under its own closer and latches shut; the gaps and any
daylight; seals, hinges, the leaf, the frame and any glazing; signage; and
whether the door is obstructed.

Two lines exist because of what actually turns up on flat entrance doors and on
double doors. One asks whether the door is free from unauthorised alterations —
**letterplates, vents, cat flaps and cabling** are the ones people fit without
thinking of the door as fire-resisting. The other asks, for a double door,
whether both leaves close in the right order and meet correctly, which a single
"does it close" question misses entirely.

The general treatment of fire door specification and installation is dealt with
in BS 8214. Nothing from that standard, or from any other standard, is reproduced
here or in the document: this is a condition-checking aid, not an implementation
of a code of practice.

## "Could not see" is an answer

"Not applicable" and "could not check" are different things. If you could not see
or reach something — a concealed seal, the far side of a flat entrance door, a
door you had no access to — write that in the notes rather than leaving the line
blank or ticking it. A gap in the record is more useful than a false tick,
because it tells the next person where to look.

## Urgent defects

A door that will not close is not a paperwork matter. The document carries a
separate instruction to report immediately any fire door that does not close, is
significantly damaged, has been removed, cannot be secured where required, or may
materially affect the safety of occupants — following the premises' own
escalation arrangements, and preventing unsafe reliance on the door until
competent advice or repair is obtained. Every defect goes in the defects and
actions log as well as on the door's own sheet.

## If your building is a block of flats in England

The Fire Safety (England) Regulations 2022 place additional duties on responsible
persons for certain multi-occupied residential buildings, and two of them are
worth keeping apart.

The first is a set of **checking duties** covering communal fire doors and flat
entrance doors. These apply to buildings above a height threshold, which the
Regulations measure by reference to the height of the top storey.

The second is a **duty to give residents information** about fire doors — keeping
them shut, not tampering with self-closing devices, and reporting faults. That
duty is **not limited by the height threshold** that governs the checking duties,
and reaches more widely across relevant multi-occupied residential buildings with
common parts.

This template is a general routine visual-check aid. It is not a statement of
either duty and does not discharge either of them. Confirm what applies to your
own building against
<LegislationLink slug="fire-safety-england-regulations-2022">the Regulations</LegislationLink>
themselves and the current official government fire door guidance.

## Available formats

The PDF is the one to print. It is A4 portrait, ten pages: the notice and the
panels that go with it on page one, then **one door to a page**, with the defects
and actions log last. Print as many door sheets as you have doors — a twenty-door
building prints twenty sheets, which is what a per-door record honestly is.

One door per page is a deliberate choice rather than a generous one. A fifteen-row
table with a header block above it and a conclusion block below it does not fit
twice on an A4 page without shrinking the rows below a height you can write in,
or splitting the table into side-by-side columns that wreck the reading order for
anyone using a screen reader. Neither is worth doing. The result is also the
better document: each sheet is a self-contained record of one door.

The Word file is the same document, editable, for anyone who needs to adapt the
premises details, the door-type options or the wording before printing. There is
no spreadsheet version: this is a form that is read on site and filled in by hand,
not a grid that is totalled.

## Accessibility

The PDF is tagged, with a document title, en-GB language metadata and a real
heading structure. The notice on page one is real text in the reading order,
marked up as a heading and paragraphs rather than an image of text. The panels
beside it each carry their own heading and sit next to the notice in the reading
order rather than after the checklist, and the panel about checking duties and the
panel about information to residents are separate structures — a merged reading
order would make the height threshold appear to govern both, which it does not.

Each per-door table has header cells with scope and a caption naming the door
reference, so the tables are distinguishable to a screen-reader user. The Y, N and
N/A responses are three separately labelled controls, the conclusion questions
below each table sit in the same tab order as the rows above them, and no state is
conveyed by colour or shading alone. The notes column has real width, for
handwriting and for magnified viewing. The Word file carries the same title,
language and heading structure.

There is no printable HTML version of this resource at present. This is a
resource that would suit one — it is used on site, often on a phone, walking a
